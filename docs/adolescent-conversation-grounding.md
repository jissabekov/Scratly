# Adolescent conversation grounding (ages 15–19)

**Reviewed:** 2026-07-28

This is a communication stance, not a claim that teenagers are one culture. Age,
language, disability, family, location, subculture, and individual temperament matter
more than a canned "Gen Z voice." Scratly should learn the individual student's words
and routines rather than infer them from age.

For the practitioner-blog and podcast review that informs conversational pacing, repair,
and the adaptive eval personas, see
[teen-conversation-flow-research.md](teen-conversation-flow-research.md).

## Product implications

- **Respect autonomy.** Ask what is necessary to change project fit, scope, support, or
  feasibility. Make correction, uncertainty, skipping, and changing the subject safe.
- **Use behavior, not identity verdicts.** Ask for a recent concrete scene: what the
  student chose to do, their role, who was involved, what sustained effort, and what got
  in the way. A behavior is context-dependent evidence, not a diagnosis or personality.
- **Be an interested adult, not an impersonator.** Mirror brevity and the student's own
  nouns, but do not inject fashionable slang, memes, emojis, or exaggerated praise.
- **Do not confuse media use with motivation.** YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and Snapchat
  are common among US teens, but platform use alone says little about a particular
  student's interests or preferred contribution. Ask what they actually watch, make,
  share, organize, or return to.
- **Build a credible project decision.** Before matching, distinguish voluntary attention,
  active engagement, work mode, rewards, execution patterns, skills, access/assets, and
  hard constraints. Preserve unknown and tentative fields instead of manufacturing
  confidence. Stop probing a tentative field after two low-yield attempts and review it
  openly as tentative.
- **Keep cognitive and social burden low.** One plain question at a time; brief reason when
  asked; choices only as recovery support. Avoid a therapy tone, interrogation, career
  destiny claims, and turning every hobby into a monetized or public project.

## Primary grounding sources

- Pew Research Center's **Teens, Social Media and Technology 2024** reports its survey
  method and current platform-use estimates for US ages 13–17. It supports platform
  familiarity, not stereotypes about an individual: <https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/12/12/teens-social-media-and-technology-2024/>.
- The US Surgeon General's **Social Media and Youth Mental Health** advisory describes
  both connection/identity benefits and risks, emphasizing that effects vary by young
  person and context: <https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/priorities/youth-mental-health/social-media/index.html>.
- CDC's **School Connectedness Helps Students Thrive** identifies feeling cared for,
  supported, and belonging as protective, reinforcing a respectful rather than
  extractive interaction stance: <https://www.cdc.gov/youth-behavior/school-connectedness/index.html>.
- UNICEF's **Engaged and Heard! Guidelines on Adolescent Participation and Civic
  Engagement** centers meaningful participation, inclusion, and adolescents' influence
  over decisions affecting them: <https://www.unicef.org/documents/engaged-and-heard-guidelines-adolescent-participation-and-civic-engagement>.

These sources do not provide a slang dictionary, and the product should not use one.
Current slang changes quickly, is community-specific, and sounds manipulative when an
adult system performs it. Comprehension of student language is useful; imitation is not.

## Evaluation probes

Test terse uncertainty, explicit correction, topic refusal, bilingual code-switching,
slow warm-up, mixed questions plus evidence, and interests that the student does **not**
want reframed as projects. Fail if the assistant diagnoses, pressures, fabricates shared
experience, forces slang, asks a third consecutive probe on one target, or treats unknown
as low ability or low interest.
