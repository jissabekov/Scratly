# Teen conversation-flow research notes

**Applied:** 2026-07-28  
**Audience:** product, prompt, and evaluation authors

This review combines practitioner articles with long-form podcast formats. It is not a
slang guide. The useful lesson across sources is how adults create enough safety and space
for a teenager to say something real without turning the exchange into an interview.

## Reading and listening set

### Practitioner articles and guides

- [UNICEF Parenting — 11 tips for communicating with your teen](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-care/11-tips-communicating-your-teen)
  emphasizes attentive listening, validation, avoiding judgment, and noticing ordinary
  chances to connect rather than forcing a formal talk.
- [Child Mind Institute — Tips for communicating with your teen](https://childmind.org/article/tips-communicating-with-teen/)
  distinguishes listening from immediately fixing and recommends making room for the
  young person's point of view.
- [Center for Parent and Teen Communication — Communicating with teens](https://parentandteen.com/communicating-with-teens/)
  frames respect, listening, and adolescent strengths as the basis for ongoing dialogue.
- [Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers — Understanding MI](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing)
  supplies the relevant discipline: partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation,
  reflections, and autonomy rather than persuasion or expert pronouncements.

### Podcasts and long-form conversations

- [Ask Lisa: The Psychology of Parenting](https://drlisadamour.com/resources/podcast/)
  models short, concrete explanations of adolescent behavior and treats feelings as valid
  without making every feeling the final decision.
- [Raising Good Humans](https://draliza.com/podcast/) uses repair, curiosity, emotional
  regulation, and connection as recurring tools instead of control scripts.
- [The Puberty Podcast](https://lessawkward.com/podcast/) demonstrates direct language
  about awkward subjects without euphemism, alarm, or a fake teen persona.

The product inference from these sources is ours: Scratly is not a parent or therapist,
but the same interaction principles reduce defensiveness during project discovery.

## Conversation rhythm to implement

1. **Low-pressure contract.** Explain the useful outcome and give permission to be unsure
   or correct the system.
2. **Follow voluntary energy.** Start with something the student actually did, watched,
   made, fixed, practiced, organized, or kept returning to. Do not translate it immediately
   into a career or product.
3. **Reflect selectively.** A short reflection should show the point understood; parroting
   every answer sounds clinical. Reflect especially after correction or a sensitive limit.
4. **One useful follow-up.** Deepen once with a recent scene or mechanism, then widen. Return
   later only when new context makes it meaningful.
5. **Explain relevance on demand.** Say how a fact changes scope, teammates, support, or
   viable projects, then offer a lighter next step.
6. **Treat resistance as information.** Uncertainty, jokes, terseness, correction, and topic
   changes alter the strategy; they are not negative assessment scores.
7. **Ask permission around exposure.** Public sharing, outreach, location, family access,
   and identity have stakes. Collect the least precise fact that changes feasibility.
8. **Offer a tentative read.** Separate strong, tentative, and unknown findings and invite
   edits before matching.
9. **Present real choices.** Give two or three meaningfully different grounded directions,
   explain fit and demands, then let the student combine, reject, or rescope them.
10. **Close with agency.** Turn the choice into a small first milestone; do not declare a
    destiny or keep assessing after the decision is sufficient.

## Anti-patterns now measured

- fixed scripts answering a question the assistant did not ask;
- three consecutive probes on one target;
- stacked questions, abstract surveys, or long adult monologues;
- slang copied from the student by the assistant;
- unearned praise, mind-reading, diagnosis, or therapy framing;
- correction without ownership, or returning to a rejected premise;
- options before review, generic options without fit evidence, or only one "choice";
- project presentation without student feedback and a scoped next step.

## Eval-design consequence

Most scenarios remain fixed probes because reproducibility helps isolate failures. Three
new adaptive end-to-end personas instead read the assistant's committed target and choose
a persona-consistent answer to the actual question. They have multiple truthful facts per
dimension, set a boundary when asked to repeat themselves, react to project options, and
stop on completion. This exposes flow failures without a second LLM that might drift,
coach the assistant, or hide nondeterminism.
